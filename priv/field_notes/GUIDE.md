# Field notes: editorial guide

The rules for every note on a major's page ("Is a ___ degree worth it?").
Both prompts include this file, so a change here changes the next run of
research and writing. Revise it as reviews teach us more; git keeps the history.

## What a note is for

The page already shows the computed answer: median first-year earnings, the
middle half, median debt and the major's rank. The note tells a student and
their parents what those numbers hide: why the best-paying programs pay
more, why the worst pay less, and whether those reasons apply to them.

## Shape

Two paragraphs, two or three sentences each.

1. **The overview and the top end.** How the major starts out against other
   majors, and what drives the best-paying programs.
2. **The low end and the downsides.** What drives the lowest-paying programs,
   and anything else a student should weigh against the major.

## The questions every note answers

Research looks for each; the note uses what it finds and skips what it can't
support.

- **Reputation or major?** How much of the top end comes from the school
  (selectivity, recruiting pipelines to banks, consulting and tech firms)
  rather than from the major itself. The facts compare the top programs'
  median admission rate with the major's (`ends`). Economics' top programs
  admit almost no one: that pay reflects who those schools admit and who
  recruits there.
- **Where?** Whether the top or bottom programs cluster in one state or
  region, and why (`ends.top_states`, `outliers`). Nursing's top programs are
  nearly all in California, which pays nurses the most. A cluster is one
  pattern; say so once.
- **A stepping stone?** Whether many graduates go on to graduate or
  professional school (biology to medicine, psychology to a counseling or
  clinical degree). First-year earnings understate such a major, and the
  note says so when research supports it.
- **Not comparable?** Programs whose students aren't new graduates (working
  professionals finishing a degree) or whose jobs differ from the major's
  usual path. These explain outliers a student won't reach.

## Naming schools

Generalize: "the most selective private universities", "programs in the
Bay Area", "campuses in Puerto Rico". Name a school only when something
specific to it explains its figure, and a student should know it:

- it admits only working professionals (SIUC's fire service track);
- its graduates do a different job (Cal Maritime's licensed marine engineers);
- its figure looks wrong, or is pooled with another campus's.

Never list the top three schools for their own sake.

## Numbers and verdicts

- No number that isn't in the facts; the checker rejects the note.
  Prefer words: the page shows the figures.
- No verdict: not "worth it", "not worth it" or "a smart choice". The data
  covers one year after graduating.
- A claim from outside the Scorecard (state pay levels, graduate school
  rates) must come from the research, with its source.

## Voice

Plain and direct, for a high-school senior and their parents. Active voice,
short sentences, one idea each. No "when it comes to", no "it's important to
note", no "not X, but Y" contrasts, no dashes as punctuation. Each note opens
on its own finding, never on a general line about the spread.
